## P01 — image

Proposed author: Max Charniuk

A post can start with a question the team has already had to answer.

Write down the customer question, the decision it prompted, the trade-off the team accepted, and why that choice matters to a buyer. Pick the format after you know the point.

Suppose a product team is choosing between another configuration option and a clearer default workflow. The useful story is the decision, the constraint behind it and the person affected—not a feature announcement on its own.

A simple capture note is: question → decision → trade-off → buyer implication → next question. It gives a writer something concrete to shape and makes it easier to preserve the founder’s reasoning.

Before opening the editor, write one customer question, the decision it prompted and the trade-off.

## P02 — image

Proposed author: Mykhailo Amplieiev

“We use AI” names a category. A buyer still needs to know what changes in their work.

Try three questions: What goes in? What does the product do? Which task or decision becomes different for the customer?

For example, “AI automates support” is broad. “The system groups incoming requests by intent” names a mechanism. “A support lead can review recurring failure themes before planning the next release” connects that mechanism to a job. Treat this as a wording example; use it only if it describes the product you are writing about.

The goal is not more technical jargon. Choose the detail that helps an intended buyer judge fit. When the mechanism is unclear, get the product explanation before choosing the words.

## P03 — image

Proposed author: Max Charniuk

A feature list tells a buyer what exists. A decision story explains why it took this shape.

Ask: Which constraint mattered? What options were considered? What evidence informed the choice? What cost did the team accept? Who benefits?

Imagine a workflow tool choosing one clear default instead of adding more settings. The story becomes useful when it explains the real constraint and the user affected. It gets more honest when it also names who might prefer the other option.

That unresolved edge can be the best part of the post: what evidence would change the decision, or which user needs more control? It gives readers something to evaluate instead of asking them to applaud a feature.

Add one line about who might choose differently when you explain a product decision.

## P04 — image

Proposed author: Mykhailo Amplieiev

A polished founder quote can sound memorable and still leave out the useful thinking.

In an interview, ask what changed recently, what the team expected before seeing the evidence, what changed their mind, which option they rejected and what remains uncertain. Then ask who needs to understand the decision.

These questions help recover the reasoning behind the quote. Keep the technical meaning intact, separate evidence from interpretation, and leave confidential details out. If the source does not support a result, tell the story of the question or method instead.

A strong follow-up is often “Can you walk me through the decision?” rather than “Can you make that sound more dramatic?”

For your next interview, choose three prompts and identify who can confirm the facts.

## P07 — image

Proposed author: Max Charniuk

Technical detail earns its place when it helps a buyer judge fit, risk or a next step.

Try a two-layer explanation. First, describe what the system does in concrete terms. Then explain what that lets the buyer decide, notice or control.

For example, imagine a tool that groups incoming records by a chosen rule. An operator might inspect exceptions before taking action. That describes a mechanism and a possible use; it does not establish that the tool saves time or improves accuracy. Those outcomes need evidence and a defined measure.

The editing question is not whether a sentence can sound less technical. Ask which detail helps the intended buyer evaluate the product. Keep the useful noun, explain the mechanism, and stop where the evidence stops.

## P18 — text-only

Proposed author: Mykhailo Amplieiev

A tidy row can still be impossible to reproduce without its source trail.

In the V5 LinkedIn pipeline, a post’s identity, capture and interpretation stay connected but separate. The source activity and canonical URL identify the post. The raw capture keeps its content hash and observation time. The parser version records how fields were read. The interpretation adds editorial meaning without changing the source record.

Python handles bounded collection and transformation steps; SQLite stores operational records and the joined analytical release. If a page is re-read, parsing the same saved bytes can create a new derivative, but it does not become a new observation.

A useful audit trail answers four questions: Which source? Which bytes? When were they captured? Which interpretation used them? Without those answers, a dashboard may hide missing lineage.

Start with one post and trace its URL, activity ID, raw hash, capture time, parser version and review row.
