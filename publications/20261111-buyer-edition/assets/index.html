The product choice buyers need to understand

Full caption / transcript
A feature list can tell a buyer what your product does. It rarely tells them why the product works this way. Take an approval step in an AI workflow. A buyer may want to know whether a person can review an uncertain result before it affects the next action. The useful story is the actual choice behind that step: what the team needed to protect, what other route it considered, and what trade-off came with the decision. That reasoning helps a reader judge whether the product fits the work they need to do. It also gives a founder something specific to explain, instead of another round of broad claims about innovation or efficiency. At Outspoken, we help founders bring this reasoning into view through interviews, original writing and a founder review of the facts and voice. The approval-step example is illustrative. Try this with one feature: write down the buyer's question, the real decision behind it, and the evidence that supports your explanation. Leave any missing answer open.

What a LinkedIn sample can tell you

Full caption / transcript
A median can describe a sample. It cannot tell you what your next post will achieve. We reviewed 279 selected posts from 61 accounts. Reaction counts were available for 276 posts, with a median of 107.5. Comment counts were available for 256, with a median of 36.5. The counters were recorded on 22 October 2026. Those denominators matter. Three posts had no observed reaction count; 23 had no observed comment count. The sample was selected, and the accounts, audiences, subjects and post ages differ. These are historical observations, not a live census of LinkedIn. Most of all, reactions and comments are interactions. They do not reveal impressions, whether the right buyers saw a post, or whether a conversation became a sale. Treating 107.5 as a target would turn a description into advice the data cannot support. A more useful next step is to choose a decision you can actually study. For example: among posts with similar audience, subject and age, what kinds of questions appear in the comments? That still would not prove what caused the difference, but it could help you decide what to inspect next. Keep the count, denominator, date and unknowns beside the takeaway. That is how a number stays useful to a buyer or founder making a real decision.

Ask for the decision, not just the topic

Full caption / transcript
“Tell me about your product” is a wide-open prompt. It can produce a polished description and still miss the part a buyer needs to understand: how the team made a choice. A more focused interview starts with a real decision. What was the team trying to make possible? What constraint mattered? Which other option was genuinely on the table? What information did the team have at the time? Those questions do not require a dramatic launch story. They help a founder explain ordinary, consequential judgment: why a workflow asks for a review, why an integration came first, or why a product leaves a step in the user's hands. Those are illustrative prompts, not claims about a specific company. Then keep the timeline honest. The choice is one fact; what happened afterward is another; the founder's explanation today may include hindsight. A good draft does not flatten them into “we knew this would work.” At Outspoken, we use interviews to find the decision and original writing to make its reasoning clear. The founder reviews the details and voice before sharing. For your next draft, pick one decision and collect the goal, constraint, plausible alternative and confirmed consequence. If a part is unknown, leave it out until you can verify it.

Four questions that make a product statement clearer

Full caption / transcript
If your product sentence could fit almost any AI company, it may be missing the information a buyer uses to decide. Try four checks. Who is the buyer in this situation? What task are they trying to complete? What would they do without your product? Which supported difference could matter to that choice? Consider the illustrative line “AI for better operations.” It does not say whose operations, what work changes, or how a buyer should assess the claim. An illustrative alternative might be: “Flag incoming requests that need a human review under a stated rule.” That gives the reader a task and a boundary to examine. It still needs a real buyer, a real alternative and product evidence before it can represent a specific company. This is not a formula for a perfect positioning statement. It is a way to notice what the first sentence asks the buyer to guess. If the buyer is unclear, adding another adjective will not supply the missing context. We built our first ten publication drafts around technical labels and boxes. In revision, we saw how those choices made the ideas less useful to a buyer. Now we start with the buyer question and make every line earn its place. Take one sentence from your site and mark the buyer, task, alternative and evidence. The blank spaces show where you need facts or a sharper choice—not a stronger promise.

Show what a product demonstration proves

Full caption / transcript
A product demo can make a capability visible. It cannot, by itself, tell a buyer how reliably that capability works across real cases. Imagine an illustrative AI tool that classifies an incoming request and flags some cases for review. A short demonstration could show one request, the output and the review step. That is useful: a buyer can see what the interaction looks like and ask whether it fits their workflow. But one example does not tell us how often the classification is right, what happens on unusual inputs, how much time the workflow takes, or whether people use it as intended. Those questions need a defined set of cases, a suitable comparison and evidence from the conditions that matter. If the product is not measured that way, say so plainly. That boundary helps the founder, too. A specific demo is easier to discuss than a sweeping claim about accuracy or savings. A buyer can ask which cases to inspect before relying on it. In our first ten drafts, technical diagrams often took attention away from the buyer’s question. We revised the work around a more practical distinction: what can I see in this example, and what evidence would I need before relying on it? When you show a feature, name the example, the conditions and what remains untested. Let the demonstration earn interest; let a benchmark carry the broader claim.

Explain what changes in the buyer’s work

Full caption / transcript
A feature name is only the beginning of a buyer's question. “Automated triage” sounds useful, but what would the person actually do differently on Monday morning? Imagine an illustrative support inbox that sorts routine requests and flags unclear ones for a person. The buyer can picture the handoff: who reviews the flagged request, and what happens next? A useful product sentence might be, “When a request is unclear, the support lead can review it before a reply goes out.” That is more useful than calling the feature “intelligent”: it shows the task and the person responsible for the next step. The example is illustrative, not a claim about a particular product. Our work at Outspoken follows the same principle: interview the founder, write an original explanation, then review the facts and voice together. The detail matters when a buyer can see what it means for their work. Try finishing this sentence: “When this feature works as described, the person responsible can…” Then check each word against the product. If the answer is still vague, you have found the next question for the product team—not a reason to embellish the copy.

Keep the founder’s voice in the final draft

Full caption / transcript
A polished post can still sound wrong if the founder would not say it that way. That does not mean every sentence needs to stay exactly as first spoken. Editing can remove repetition, explain a term and give the reader a clearer route through the idea. But the founder's actual position, the strength of the claim and the meaning of the product choice must survive the edit. A practical review is to read the draft aloud and check three things: Is this what I believe? Can I support this detail? Would I explain the decision this way to a buyer? If the answer is no, revise the line rather than asking the founder to perform a voice that is not theirs. At Outspoken, we pair original writing with a founder interview and a review of facts and voice. The finished post should be clearer while still sounding like the person whose judgment it represents. The reader benefits as well. A real point of view is easier to evaluate than polished copy that could belong to any company. The goal is not to preserve every rough edge; it is to make the founder's actual thinking clear. When you review your next post, circle the sentence that carries the strongest claim. Confirm that it is true, yours, and phrased at a level you can stand behind. Then edit for clarity around it.

Make the visual answer a buyer’s question

Full caption / transcript
A diagram is not useful just because it has boxes and arrows. It earns its place when it helps someone understand a choice, compare two real options or inspect a step in the work. For example, an illustrative AI workflow could show where a result goes to human review and what happens next. That visual may help a buyer see who remains responsible. A decorative sequence of “input → AI → impact” would tell them much less, especially if the labels hide the actual decision. We saw this in our own first ten publication drafts: technical boxes and labels obscured the buyer’s question. We revised around a clearer test: can the reader see what this visual helps them decide? Sometimes the answer is a small annotated example. Sometimes it is a clear sentence and no diagram at all. A visual should carry a specific part of the explanation, while the caption adds context and the limits. Before you turn a product idea into a graphic, write the buyer's question at the top of the page. If the image cannot help answer it, simplify the image or choose a different form. Clear communication is the aim; better business outcomes need their own evidence.

Choose a business question before choosing a metric

Full caption / transcript
It is easy to count reactions. It is harder to decide what those reactions mean for your business. Start with the decision. Are you trying to understand whether a post reached the people you intended, whether the explanation made sense, or whether a prospect wants to discuss the problem? Those are different questions, and each needs different evidence. Reactions alone cannot answer all three. Think about three practical questions: Did the post reach the intended people? Did they understand the product choice? Did a relevant prospect want to discuss it? Each needs its own evidence: reach data, a way to check comprehension, or a real conversation in context. A reaction count cannot stand in for all three. That distinction changes what to do next. If the question is reach, you need reach data. If it is comprehension, you need a way to assess whether readers understand the point. If it is commercial interest, you need to observe relevant conversations and their context. Do not treat one visible counter as a substitute for the evidence you lack. For your next review, write the decision first, then list the evidence that could inform it and the gaps that remain. A metric is useful when it is connected to a question you can answer—not when it merely looks precise.
