01-the-reason · carousel

Make the thinking visible

Download caption
Read draft caption

A founder can explain a product perfectly and still leave the buyer with one uncomfortable question: why should this matter to me? I think the missing part is often the reasoning behind the product. The decision to solve one problem first. The alternative the team ruled out. The detail that makes a workflow difficult in practice. That is where I want Outspoken to start: with the founder’s thinking and the buyer’s questions. We shape the position, write the story and create the visual. The founder checks the claims before anything is ready to share. The result we are aiming for is clearer communication that gives the right people a reason to care. Reach and sales are outcomes to measure, not promises we can make from a draft. What do buyers need to understand before your product makes sense?

02-what-we-actually-saw · carousel

What our research can and cannot tell us

Download caption
Read draft caption

We reviewed a selected set of 279 LinkedIn posts from 61 accounts. The number I would not turn into a target: 107.5. That was the median reaction count among the 276 posts with an observed reaction count. For comments, the median was 36.5 across 256 posts with an observed comment count. Those are different sets of posts. A reaction can be a like or another reaction type. It is not a comment, an impression or a sale. We did not have reach or conversion data for this sample. So I would rather ask what a post explained, what evidence it used, and whether that question matters to our buyer. Outspoken uses research to find ideas worth testing—not a formula for guaranteed results. The sample was selected; counters were captured 20–22 October 2026. It is not a LinkedIn benchmark. 03-the-interview · carousel

The sentence hiding inside a decision

Download caption
Read draft caption

The sentence a founder needs for a post may not arrive as a polished line. It may appear when you ask: what did you decide, what else did you consider, and what changed your mind? I would start an Outspoken interview there. Not to manufacture a dramatic origin story, but to understand a decision the founder can explain accurately. Then we connect that decision to a question a buyer faces. A useful draft needs the trade-off, the evidence that can be shared, and a clear point for the reader. The founder reviews the final wording and decides what remains private. If you could explain one decision behind your company to a future buyer, which would it be?

04-positioning · carousel

If everyone can say it

Download caption
Read draft caption

If every company in your category can make the same claim, the buyer still has the work of figuring out the difference. Take a generic line like “AI-powered workflows.” It describes a broad category. It does not tell me which buyer it serves, which job it supports, or why your approach is relevant. This is an illustrative phrase, not a customer quotation. A more useful starting point is a sequence: name the buyer, describe the job, then explain a difference you can substantiate. That is how I want Outspoken to approach positioning before we turn it into LinkedIn copy and visuals. A sharper sentence should make the next conversation easier to understand. Its commercial effect still needs to be measured. Which phrase on your page could a competitor use unchanged?

05-proof · carousel

A bold claim needs a boundary

Download caption
Read draft caption

A strong claim is easier to trust when the reader can see its edges. Suppose a product helps a team review incoming requests. Before saying it “automates the work,” I would want to know: which requests, what does the system return, and where does a person still need to check the result? This is a hypothetical example. Those details do more than make a post precise. They give a buyer something concrete to evaluate. Our proposed process at Outspoken is to map the claim to its source, write the useful explanation, and return it to the founder for a factual check. If the evidence is not available, we say so or hold the claim. What is one claim about your product that deserves a clearer boundary?

06-product-to-buyer · single

Turn a feature into a decision

Download caption
Read draft caption

Here is a writing exercise I would use with an AI founder: explain a feature without starting with the model. Start with the moment a person needs to act. What triggers the work? What information does the product use? What does it return? Who checks the result—and what can that person do next? For example, a workflow diagram could show a request coming in, an AI system suggesting an answer, and a person reviewing it before it reaches a customer. That is an illustration, not a claim about a particular product. Once the sequence is clear, we can choose what belongs in a post, a visual or a short script. The founder checks the technical detail and what can be shared. Which part of your product is hardest to explain without using its internal terminology?

07-two-voices · single

One story, two useful voices

Download caption
Read draft caption

A founder and a company page do not need to post the same sentence. Give each voice a job around the same buyer question. The founder can explain a decision and the trade-off behind it. The company can explain the workflow, show the product context, or share evidence that has been cleared for use. That is an editorial approach I would test, not a claim that one kind of account automatically performs better. The right division depends on the founder, the team and the audience they want to reach. At Outspoken, we can help shape that shared story and develop separate drafts for each voice. Where does your company story need a founder’s judgment—and where does it need a clearer product explanation?

08-visuals · single

A useful visual answers a question

Download caption
Read draft caption

A beautiful visual can still leave me asking: what am I supposed to understand? Before designing a LinkedIn image, I would choose one question the reader should be able to answer after seeing it. If the post explains a workflow, show the sequence. If it compares two approaches, make the difference visible. If it makes a claim, put the source or limitation close enough to read. That is the role we want visuals to play at Outspoken: give a founder’s idea a form a buyer can follow. The design supports the argument; it does not verify the claim for us. What should a reader understand in five seconds from your next visual?

09-measure · single

A reaction is not a sale

Download caption
Read draft caption

A reaction is a signal. It is not a sale. A reaction count combines likes and other reaction types. Comments are a separate measure. Neither tells us how many people saw the post, visited a website or became a buyer. When we reviewed our selected sample, those distinctions mattered: reactions were observable on 276 of 279 posts and comments on 256. The sample’s counters were captured 20–22 October 2026 and cannot establish conversions or what is trending today. For a client, I would start with the question we want the content to answer, record the measures we can actually observe, and keep missing data marked as unknown. Then we can decide what to test next. Which question are your current LinkedIn numbers helping you answer? Source: https://github.com/mcharniuk1-spec/linkedin/tree/main/reports/20261105
