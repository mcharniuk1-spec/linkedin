Someone read your post. What happens next?

They open your profile. Can they see who you help? They look for an example. Is there something useful to inspect? They reply. Does the conversation have somewhere to go?

At OutSpoken, we build that path around the founder’s knowledge:

• Learn the product, buyer and voice. Turn the interview, decks and existing material into a clear brief and profile.
• Build the content around real buyer questions. Explain a decision, demonstrate a useful approach or answer an objection.
• Review, publish and participate. The founder checks the facts and position; the post starts a conversation we can actually respond to.
• With Voice + Pipeline, research relevant people, open useful conversations and qualify the replies before arranging a meeting.
• Carry the context into sales. The next person should know why the buyer agreed to talk.

For example, an AI reporting company could build a post around “How do I check an answer before using it?” Its profile leads to a worked example. A relevant conversation can then start with the reader’s reporting task.

The reader leaves with something usable: an explanation, an example to inspect, or an answer to their own problem. Each step gives the next conversation a starting point.

02-the-strategyFounder / marketer

A content pillar is not a topic label

“AI”, “leadership” and “our journey” are topics. What is a buyer supposed to do with them?

Build the strategy around three decisions the buyer needs to make:

1. Is this problem worth solving? Explain the cost of the current workflow, with a concrete example.
2. Is this approach right for us? Show the alternatives and the reason for your choice.
3. Can we put it to work? Explain setup, responsibility and the first useful result someone can check.

Take an illustrative invoice-approval product. A useful sequence could be a checklist for finding approval delays, a comparison of rule-based and manual routing, then a walkthrough of one invoice from arrival to approval.

Now the founder’s opinion has a job. The designer can show a choice. The developer can explain a trade-off. The buyer can see where the product fits.

Put those three decisions into a simple content board. Beside every idea, add the buyer’s question, the material you can show, the owner and the next step.

For OutSpoken, that board is the working brief. It tells the writer what to answer and the founder what evidence to bring.

03-the-interviewFounder / non-technical team

One founder conversation can do more than produce quotes

A founder interview should leave you with material you can use.

The interview needs to produce four things:

• A decision: what did you choose, and what made the alternative less suitable?
• A buyer question: what does someone need to understand before they can choose?
• A useful example: what can we show or explain without exposing a client’s information?
• Your position: what would you challenge, recommend or refuse to promise?

Imagine a founder explaining why their onboarding asks for a sample before connecting live company data. That can become a post about the buyer’s risk, a short walkthrough of the first session and a practical checklist for evaluating a trial.

One interview can support several posts when each answers a different buyer question.

A shared document is enough to start. Put the four headings at the top, capture the founder’s actual words underneath, and use comments or suggestions to resolve factual edits.

Our Voice offer is built around one interview every two weeks and three posts a week. Each draft must answer its own buyer question and preserve the founder’s reasoning.

04-the-profileFounder / consultant

Your profile has to finish the job your post started

A good post sends someone to your profile. What do they find there?

If the headline says “Founder | AI | Innovation”, the buyer still has to work out whether you help someone like them.

Run this profile check before doing more outreach:

• Headline: say who you work with and what work you help them do.
• About: explain the problem, your approach and what working together involves.
• Featured: give the visitor one useful example or a clear next step.
• Recent posts: let them hear your judgment, not just read your job title.

For OutSpoken, a proposed headline is: “Building OutSpoken | LinkedIn content and buyer conversations for Europe-based B2B AI SaaS founders.”

The wording is only the start. The rest of the profile has to make it credible: a clear service explanation, practical examples and a way to talk.

The profile should finish the explanation the post started. If the post offers a practical workflow, make that workflow easy to find in Featured.

Open your own profile as a buyer. What can they understand in the first screen, and what do they still have to guess?

05-the-toolsFounder / small team

Choose the jobs before choosing the tool stack

Before adding another LinkedIn tool, name the job it will do.

A small founder-led programme has six useful jobs:

• Capture knowledge: a shared document for interview notes, product facts and examples.
• Plan the content: a simple board with the buyer question, owner and review date.
• Draft and review: a writing workflow that preserves the founder’s position; document suggestions make changes visible.
• Preview and publish: native LinkedIn tools, with a preview tool if you need to check line breaks before posting.
• Research people: native search or Sales Navigator when its filters are useful.
• Keep the conversation: a spreadsheet or CRM record with the actual reply and agreed next action.

Google Docs, AuthoredUp, Sales Navigator and HubSpot can cover parts of that list. They serve different functions; you do not need to buy them all to start.

The client’s side should be equally clear: supply the product material, join the interview and review the claims. Any account access or subscription needs a named owner and a reason.

Our OutSpoken stack should make those handoffs easier. Each subscription needs a task, an owner and a cost we can explain.

06-the-first-messageFounder / sales

Give someone a reason to reply

Would you reply to “Thanks for connecting. We help companies grow”?

A first message should make the reason for writing obvious. A useful structure is:

• A detail you actually checked.
• The problem that makes that detail relevant.
• One small, useful next step.

Illustrative example, only if the public hiring detail is real:

“Hi Maya — I saw you’re hiring your first customer-success lead. We put together a short checklist for turning recurring onboarding questions into useful founder posts. Would that be relevant while you build the role?”

The sender has offered something connected to the recipient’s work. Maya can answer without committing to a meeting.

Start the research with a clear role, company type and location. Sales Navigator can help narrow that search; then read the actual company and person context before drafting the note.

At OutSpoken, the content and outreach need the same buyer understanding. If our posts address one problem and our messages pitch another, the conversation loses its thread.

What useful thing could you offer before asking someone for calendar time?

07-the-replyFounder / sales

What do you send when a prospect says “send me details”?

“Send me details” is a chance to make the next reply useful.

Sending the whole deck too early leaves the recipient to find their own answer. Try four moves:

1. Answer the request: offer a short, relevant explanation.
2. Clarify the situation: ask one question that changes what you would recommend.
3. Show the right example: send one useful artifact rather than every feature.
4. Agree the next step: let their answer guide whether a call is useful.

Illustrative reply:

“Happy to. Is the bigger problem finding ideas, or turning your existing knowledge into posts that sound like you? If it’s the second, I can send a one-page example of the interview-to-post workflow.”

That question matters because the answer changes the work. An ideas problem may need a strategy brief. A voice problem needs better source material and editing. A buyer-relevance problem needs a clearer audience and point of view.

Record the actual answer and next action in the contact’s CRM record. HubSpot supports manual activity logging; a small spreadsheet can also keep the thread visible.

What is the one question that would help you send less material and give a better answer?

09-the-buying-groupFounder / business owner

Your champion is not the whole buying group

Your product champion may understand the feature. The person approving the purchase may have a different question.

In the 2025 Edelman–LinkedIn survey, 55% of US hidden decision-makers—people who influence purchases without being the main product contact—said reviewing a company’s thought leadership was one important way they vetted vendors.

That makes me think about what a founder’s posts give the wider team:

• The user wants to know how the task changes.
• The manager wants to know what the team has to adopt.
• Finance wants a credible explanation of cost and value.
• Security or legal may need a clear account of data and responsibility.

A post cannot replace the purchasing process. It can make one of those conversations easier to have.

For an illustrative AI reporting tool, the content plan could include a worked answer, a setup checklist and an explanation of who checks the output before it is used.

Our strategy brief needs a place for those less-visible buying roles, too. Who else needs to understand the decision?

10-the-reviewFounder / manager

What should a weekly LinkedIn review change?

What would make you change next week’s content or outreach?

Put four questions into a simple review sheet:

1. Did we publish the material we planned?
2. Which people matching our agreed buyer roles and company criteria responded, and what did they actually ask?
3. Which conversations moved to an agreed next step?
4. What should we change because of that evidence?

Use native LinkedIn analytics for the distribution you can observe. Use the actual replies and CRM notes for the conversation. Keep the commercial next step visible: resource requested, problem clarified, call agreed, or no fit.

An illustrative review note might read: “An operations lead asked how the workflow handles an exception. Next post: show that case. Next conversation: ask which exception is costing their team the most effort.”

Now the report is doing work. The writer has a useful topic, the designer has a specific example, and the person replying has a relevant question.

11-the-designerDesigner / founder

A beautiful product still needs a clear first step

If a buyer has to ask what to click, the mockup still has a communication problem to solve.

When AI helps a team generate more interface options, someone still has to choose. The designer’s judgment shows up in the choice: what does the person need to understand, what should they do first, and what could go wrong?

Take an illustrative AI reporting trial. “Start your AI journey” gives the visitor a broad promise. “Try a report with sample data” explains the next action and avoids asking for company data before the visitor understands the product.

The design story is already there:

• The user needs a low-effort way to assess the output.
• The interface offers a sample task.
• The next screen shows the report and where its evidence comes from.

That is useful material for a founder’s LinkedIn post. Show the choice, explain why it matters and give the reader something they can apply to their own onboarding.

For OutSpoken, this is the job of the visual: make the choice visible. A worked interface example can tell the story faster than another broad claim about better user experience.

12-the-developerDeveloper / technical founder

Explain what makes your AI output worth trusting

“Built with AI” leaves an important buying question unanswered: why should someone trust the output?

The 2025 Stack Overflow Developer Survey reported more distrust than trust in AI-tool accuracy: 46% versus 33%, among respondents to that question.

A technical founder can address that concern with a concrete product explanation:

• What information does the answer use?
• How can a person check it?
• Which case should trigger a review or a stop?

Imagine an AI reporting feature. A useful post could show the question, the answer, the source link and one situation where the user should check the result before acting.

The developer can explain the implementation choice. The designer can make the check visible. The founder can connect it to the work the buyer is trying to do.

That gives the technical detail a purpose. It also makes the limits specific enough for someone to assess, rather than hiding them behind “reliable AI”.

13-the-managerManager / non-technical team

Give the reviewer a decision to make

“Any feedback?” is a surprisingly expensive review brief.

The writer gets a tone comment. The founder changes a product fact. Someone else rewrites the call to action. Nobody knows which decision was needed to finish the post.

Give three checks to the people reviewing the draft:

• Product truth: does the explanation match what we deliver?
• Founder voice: is this the position and wording the author can stand behind?
• Reader action: is the next step useful and part of the actual offer?

Put those questions beside the draft, name the reviewer and set the handoff. In a shared Google Doc, suggestions can show the proposed change; a comment can explain the reason.

For example: “This sentence promises a response time we have not agreed. Please confirm the service commitment or remove the time claim.” That is an actionable edit. “Make it stronger” still leaves the writer guessing.

This matters when non-technical colleagues help explain a technical product. Give them the actual product behaviour, the proposed promise and the decision you need them to make.

At OutSpoken, the founder’s review should protect the meaning and let the work move forward.
