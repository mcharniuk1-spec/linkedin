01-the-reason · carousel

Show the decision behind the product

Read full text for Show the decision behind the product

Caption and source limits

A feature list tells a buyer what exists. A decision can explain why it exists that way. Which problem did the team choose to solve first? What did it rule out? Where did it keep a person in the loop? Those details reveal priorities and trade-offs. They give a reader something more useful to assess than a list of capabilities. That is the starting point of the content workflow we are proposing at Outspoken: recover a real decision, connect it to a buyer question, check the facts, then write an original account of the reasoning. The immediate aim is clearer communication. Whether that earns attention, qualified conversations or sales is something to observe separately. Start the brief with the decision a buyer needs to understand.

02-what-we-actually-saw · carousel

A median is not a posting target

Read full text for A median is not a posting target

Caption and source limits

107.5 reactions is a number. It is not a posting target. Our saved reference sample contains 279 selected posts from 61 accounts. Reaction counts were available for 276 posts; their median was 107.5. Comment counts were available for 256; their median was 36.5. These counters were recorded on 22 October 2026. The sample spans different authors, audiences, topics and publication ages. It is not a picture of everything happening on LinkedIn now. The numbers describe observed interactions. They do not tell us how many people saw the posts, whether the right buyers cared, or whether anyone bought. Use the sample to find a question worth testing. Compare similar conditions, keep the denominator, and leave the unsupported conclusion open. A number is more useful when its limits travel with it.

03-the-interview · carousel

Find the decision in the interview

Read full text for Find the decision in the interview

Caption and source limits

A useful founder interview starts with a choice, not a topic list. Ask what the person was trying to achieve, what constrained the decision and which other option looked reasonable at the time. The rejected option should be a real alternative, not the villain hired for the story. Then separate three things: what the founder chose, what happened afterward, and how they explain it now. Those are related, but they are not interchangeable. This is a proposed interview method, not a story about a client engagement already delivered. A real post still needs the author's confirmed details and permission to share them. For the next interview, choose one decision and record the goal, alternatives and information available at the time.

04-positioning · carousel

Four checks for a useful product statement

Read full text for Four checks for a useful product statement

Caption and source limits

If your product statement could describe twenty companies, inspect what it leaves out. Start with four questions. Who is the buyer, and in what situation are they deciding? What task are they trying to complete? What would they do instead? What supported difference would change that choice? A hypothetical example: "AI for better operations" leaves the work undefined. "Flag requests for a human reviewer against a stated rule" names an action and a review boundary. It still needs a buyer, a real alternative and evidence before it becomes a complete product claim. The point is to make the decision assessable. A clearer sentence does not, by itself, prove a sales improvement. Which question does your first sentence leave unanswered: buyer, task, alternative or evidence?

05-proof · carousel

A demonstration is not a benchmark

Read full text for A demonstration is not a benchmark

Caption and source limits

A screenshot is not a time-saving claim. Imagine a request-review tool. A hypothetical demonstration could show three sample requests, a stated review rule and the flags the tool would suggest. A person would check those suggestions before any next action. That would make one part of the proposed workflow inspectable. It would not establish a typical error rate, repeated speed or a reliable customer saving. For a performance claim, define the task, the comparison, the sample, the measurement period and the human work included. Then report what was actually observed, with its operating conditions. The example here is illustrative. No product execution, benchmark or customer result is being reported. Write the claim at the level the evidence can support. A demonstration can show a step. A saving needs a comparable test.

06-product-to-buyer · single

Follow the feature through the work

Read full text for Follow the feature through the work

Caption and source limits

What happens immediately before and after the feature is used? That question can reveal the gap in a product explanation. A useful walkthrough names the trigger, input, output, reviewer and next decision. Keep the technical detail that changes how the buyer should assess the workflow. Explain the terms they need rather than stripping the mechanism away. If the example is proposed, say so. If it describes a real implementation, confirm it with the technical owner and the relevant version. An attractive diagram is not verification that the product works that way. Take one feature and sketch the path from the trigger to the next human decision. Fill the first gap a new reader would encounter.

07-two-voices · single

Two accounts need two contributions

Read full text for Two accounts need two contributions

Caption and source limits

Two accounts can answer one buyer question without publishing the same answer twice. A founder can explain why a decision was made: the constraint, the alternative and the trade-off. A company account can add the product detail, source material or demonstration that helps a reader assess it. That division is an editorial proposal. It is not a claim that founder accounts always earn more reach or leads. Start with the shared question. Then ask what distinct evidence or explanation each account can contribute. If the second post adds nothing, a different logo is not enough reason to repeat it. Give the second publication a useful job of its own.

09-measure · single

Choose the question before the metric

Read full text for Choose the question before the metric

Caption and source limits

Choose the question before you choose the metric. A reaction count can describe visible interactions. It cannot, on its own, tell you whether a buyer understood the product or wants to buy. For understanding, read the actual replies and questions. For commercial interest, look for an explicit problem and an agreed next step. Those are different observations, not meanings to assign to every comment. In the preserved 279-post sample, reaction counts were available for 276 posts and comments for 256. Missing impressions and buyer-outcome records stayed missing. Keep a simple map: the decision you need to make, the evidence you have, and the answer that remains unknown. Which decision are your current content metrics unable to support?
