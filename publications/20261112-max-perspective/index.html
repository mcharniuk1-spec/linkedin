I asked for the posts to be redone

I asked for our first ten LinkedIn publications to be redone.

They had structure, technical labels and diagrams. But I still wanted a clearer answer to a basic question: what does the reader get from this?

That became the brief for OutSpoken. Start with the problem a founder or buyer recognises. Explain one decision. Show the detail that makes it useful.

For a product post, that detail might be why the team kept a review step, which alternative it considered, or what the user has to do next. The choice gives us something to discuss. The feature name alone leaves too much work to the reader.

Now I want to use the same test on my own posts. Can someone explain the point back without knowing how we built the system behind it?

This revision is still work in progress. We have drafts and visual assets to review; we do not yet have audience results from this edition.

If you are reviewing a draft today, try one question before editing the wording: what will the reader understand or decide more clearly after this?

The question I want to ask a founder

The founder-interview question I want to start with is: which product decision was harder than it looks from the outside?

It leaves room for something more interesting than a tour of features.

What were you trying to achieve? Which constraint mattered? What other option was genuinely possible? What did you know at the time?

I want to build our writing process around those details. They help a reader follow a person’s judgment, including the part that still has uncertainty.

There is a useful difference between the decision, what happened afterward, and how you explain it now. A good story can hold all three. It does not need to pretend that the ending was obvious at the start.

This is the interview method we are developing for OutSpoken, not a report of a client interview we have already completed.

If you wanted to try it on your own product, choose one decision and write down the goal, constraint, alternative and observed consequence. Leave a blank where the evidence is missing. That blank is a much better interview prompt than another request to make the story more inspiring.

What I want a product sentence to tell me

“AI for better operations” gives me very little to work with.

Whose operations? Which task? What would that person do without the product? Why would they choose this approach?

Those are the questions I want a product sentence to begin answering. The first line does not have to explain the whole company. It should give the reader somewhere concrete to start.

Take an illustrative support workflow. “A support lead reviews unclear requests before a reply goes out” identifies a person, a task and a point of control. It is still only an example. To represent a real product, we would need to check that it works that way and understand the actual alternative.

That check matters to me. A sentence can become more specific and still be wrong.

For OutSpoken, I want positioning and publication work to share the same discipline: make the buyer’s situation visible, then explain the difference we can support.

Try marking those four parts in one sentence from your site: person, task, alternative, supported difference. The empty spaces tell you what to investigate before you reach for a stronger adjective.

What I would ask after the demo

After a product demo, I want to know which part I can rely on.

Seeing one request enter a workflow and a sensible result come out is useful. It shows me the interaction. I can ask where a person checks the result and what happens next.

Then the questions change. How were the examples selected? What happens with an unclear request? Which cases fail? How much review does the output need?

I want room for those questions in founder content. They make the explanation more useful to someone deciding whether to try the product.

An attractive demonstration can earn attention. A broader reliability claim needs evidence from a defined set of cases and the conditions that matter to the buyer. If that work has not been done, the post can say what the example shows and leave the wider question open.

That is the standard I want us to use when we explain our own tools, too. We have tool specifications and a manual rehearsal. A client-ready app and a measured result are separate deliverables.

Which case would you want to see before you trusted the workflow? That is the question I would bring to the next demonstration.

The next person in the workflow

When I read product copy, I want to understand what the next person actually does.

“Automated triage” is a feature name. I still need to understand who sees the result, what they check, and what happens when the answer is unclear.

Take an illustrative writing workflow. A founder corrects a product detail in a draft. The writer updates the caption, and the visual needs the same correction. Who checks that both versions now say the same thing? That handoff gives me something concrete to examine.

In one ArchFlow post, I wrote about what happens after information is captured: the source, the responsible person and the next action. I want to bring that clarity into OutSpoken’s writing, while keeping the two companies’ offers distinct.

The useful story may be a very ordinary handoff. That is enough if it helps the right reader understand the work.

Try this on one feature: when it works as described, who can do what next? Then ask the product team to check the sentence. If nobody can answer precisely, there is still a product conversation to have before the copy is ready.

Make it clearer, and keep it mine

I can ask for a complete rewrite and still want the finished post to sound like me.

That is exactly what I am asking for with these OutSpoken publications: more personal perspective, useful explanation, space to read and visuals that carry part of the idea.

The difficult part is deciding what the editor may change. Structure, repetition and an unclear term are fair work. My actual position, the strength of a claim and the facts of a story need to survive.

“More human” should make the person easier to hear. It should not add a convenient memory, a customer result or a feeling the author never supplied.

For my own review, I want three questions beside the draft. Is this what I mean? Can I stand behind the detail? Would I explain it this way to a person I wanted to work with?

Some answers will change the text. That is part of the writing, not a defect to hide.

If you are editing someone else’s post, ask them which sentence feels least like them. Their correction may tell you more than another page of tone-of-voice adjectives.

The arrows were not answering my question

I looked at our publication drafts and asked for the arrows and blocks to be reconsidered.

They were explaining an architecture. I wanted the reader to understand a practical choice.

That changes the design brief. A comparison can make two options easier to inspect. An annotated sentence can show what changed in the explanation. A small coverage chart can keep a number’s denominator visible.

Each of those has a job. A realistic scene can have one too: show the setting of a conversation or the work around a decision. I do not want a photo added simply because there is empty space.

So the question I want above every visual brief is: what becomes easier to understand because this image exists?

There is room for personality, surprise and good-looking work. I want those things. I also want to be able to explain why the visual belongs with this particular post.

The current revision is testing that approach within OutSpoken’s design system. It is a design decision in progress, not evidence of improved reach.

What I want to learn from the first posts

Before I set a reaction target for these posts, I want to choose what I am trying to learn.

Did the explanation make sense? Did it reach someone relevant? Did a person want to discuss the problem?

Those questions need different evidence. A reader explaining the idea back can help with comprehension. Native analytics can describe distribution when available. A relevant conversation gives us context about interest. None of them automatically proves a sale.

The saved LinkedIn sample is useful here because it shows the limit of what we can see from public counters. We have reactions and comments for many records. We do not have reach or sales.

For our own posts, I want a small review record: the reader question, the evidence we can actually observe, what surprised us and what we would change next.

That leaves room for a disappointing result without immediately blaming the format or the algorithm. It also stops a large number from doing all the thinking for us.

If you had to choose one thing to learn from your next four posts, what would it be? I would rather answer that clearly before building a dashboard around it.
