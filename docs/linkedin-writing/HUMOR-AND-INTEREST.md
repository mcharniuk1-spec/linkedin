# Humor and interest

Version 2.0 · 10 November 2026 · original editorial guidance for founder review.

Use this guide with the [writing architecture](../LINKEDIN-WRITING-ARCHITECTURE.md), [patterns P01–P14](patterns.json), [hooks and proof guide](HOOKS-PROOF-ENDINGS.md) and [source registry](source-registry.json). The techniques below are editorial synthesis. Research findings are identified separately by LWT source ID; none establishes a universal LinkedIn performance effect.

**All sample wording, dialogue, situations and first-person statements are original fictional illustrations. They are not founder memories, client incidents or quotations from source authors.** A draft about a real experience needs confirmed material and the author's voice review. Humor remains optional.

## 1. Find what is interesting about the subject

Interest can arise from a decision the reader recognises, a consequence they had overlooked or a process they can finally inspect. Start with that substance. An adjective such as “incredible” cannot supply a missing consequence.

The following mechanisms are a practical editorial classification, not a list of experimentally ranked engagement techniques.

| Mechanism | Fictional illustration | What must follow |
|---|---|---|
| A specific unanswered question | “Why does the report finish in minutes while the decision still takes days?” | Investigate the difference between producing information and making the decision. |
| A supported discrepancy | “The feature received praise in interviews and little repeated use in the pilot.” | Define the two observations and examine plausible explanations. |
| A consequential trade-off | “The faster release depended on keeping a manual repair step.” | Show who carried the work and how that affected the choice. |
| A revealing detail | “The handoff had an owner, but no acceptance criterion.” | Explain how the missing criterion affected this process. |
| An inspectable method | “Here is the input we rejected before evaluating the output.” | Reveal the actual or clearly illustrative test and its reason. |
| Recognition of a familiar tension | “Everyone could edit the dashboard. Nobody could explain the metric.” | Develop the distinction between access and responsibility without pretending it describes every team. |
| A revised interpretation | “We first treated the repeated request as a missing feature. It was a recovery problem.” | Show the evidence that justified changing the interpretation. |

A post needs enough context to make its question intelligible. The answer can appear early while the explanation carries the interest. A concrete consequence may matter more to this reader than a dramatic personal confession. Use an authentic small observation when that is the material available.

## 2. Use curiosity and detail with their limits intact

[LWT01: Loewenstein, 1994](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/PsychofCuriosity.pdf) develops an information-gap theory of curiosity. Relevant pages of the full scan were inspected. [LWT02: Kang and colleagues, 2009](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/9f1n0-x0z52) used laboratory trivia tasks and found associations between curiosity, willingness to obtain answers and later recall. Relevant methods and results were inspected; the samples were small and key curiosity relationships were correlational. Neither source tests a founder's LinkedIn opening.

The editorial application is to give readers a question worth resolving. Avoid a vague pronoun whose subject is hidden merely to delay understanding. Test the promise by writing the eventual answer in ordinary language. If that answer is thin, more suspense will not improve the underlying material.

Concrete language can make a situation easier to inspect, but its persuasive effect depends on the task. [LWT04: Packard and Berger, 2021](https://schulich.yorku.ca/faculty/grant-packard/) reports that concreteness in customer service can signal attention to customer needs. [LWT06: Schellekens, Verlegh and Smidts, 2010](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/20390) reports that abstract positive recommendations can convey a more general favourable attitude and affect purchase intention. Only the author abstracts were inspected for these two papers. Their contexts and outcomes differ.

Use a detail to establish what happened, then explain its significance. “A weekly exception required a manual repair” gives a situation; the next sentence should explain whether that frequency changes the decision. Adding a colour, date or number that changes nothing may create atmosphere, but it does not strengthen the evidence.

## 3. Let a story explain a choice

For P02 and P03, the interesting movement is often the difference between the author's original understanding and the decision eventually made. Show a goal, a constraint, an alternative and a consequence. Keep the person's knowledge inside the scene separate from today's interpretation.

[LWT07: Green and Brock, 2000](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/the-role-of-transportation-in-the-persuasiveness-of-public-narrat/) relates narrative transportation to story-consistent beliefs and character evaluations. Relevant methods and results from the full article were inspected. Studies 1–3 primarily support associations; Study 4 used an effective processing manipulation. This is not a direct demonstration that stories outperform statistics, and narrative conviction does not establish representativeness.

**Weak fictional version:** “One difficult customer taught me everything about product strategy.”

**Improved fictional version:** “The request initially looked like a feature gap. Once we traced the failed handoff, the decision became whether to improve recovery or add another option.”

The revised version gives the reader a professional problem to understand. A real version still needs the actual request, investigation and decision. The lesson should remain as narrow as that material warrants.

## 4. Understand the humor trade-off

[LWT08: Bitterly, Brooks and Schweitzer, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27831701/) reports eight studies of humor and status. Appropriate successful humor could increase status through perceptions of confidence and competence; inappropriate attempts could undermine competence and status. The full online-first article's relevant sections were inspected. Failed humor did not produce the same result in every comparison: Study 2a's failed-versus-serious comparison was not statistically significant. That does not prove no effect. It does prevent the rule that every unsuccessful joke necessarily harms status.

[LWT09: Bitterly and Schweitzer, 2019](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/The-impression-management-benefits-of-humorous-self-disclosures-How-humor-influences-perceptions-of-veracity..pdf) reports five studies in which humorous negative self-disclosure could improve perceived warmth and competence while reducing the perceived veracity of nearby negative information. The relevant full-text methods and results were inspected. Positive claims, distant statements and failed humor were not all established extensions of that finding.

These outcomes are distinct. An author can seem warmer while a particular admission seems less literal. “Humor builds trust” is therefore too broad for this evidence. The practical editorial response is to preserve the factual point independently of the joke. Whether that response improves a particular LinkedIn outcome remains untested here.

## 5. Choose an original technique that fits the voice

These are compositional options, not a validated humor taxonomy. The examples are fictional and may be unsuitable for a particular founder's tone or audience.

### Recognisable incongruity

**Illustration:** “Our single source of truth had developed a second opinion.”

The phrase puts a declared ideal beside a recognisable contradiction. It could introduce a P13 observation about conflicting definitions. The body should specify the actual disagreement if the post makes a factual claim. Do not let the phrase accuse a named team of unreliability without evidence.

### Restrained understatement

**Illustration:** “The sixth manual approval suggested that the automated workflow still enjoyed company.”

A calm description exposes friction. This can sit inside P04 or P09 when the manual steps are confirmed. The literal explanation might identify why one approval remains necessary and which others are candidates for removal. The joke does not establish that all approvals are wasteful.

### Self-recognition about a habit

**Illustration:** “I built a dashboard for every question. Finding the right dashboard became the next question.”

The author is the target, and the observation can lead into P03's revised practice. In a real account, confirm the habit and the change. Self-directed humor is not automatically harmless: jokes about a capability the reader must rely on can create ambiguity about competence.

### A bounded analogy

**Illustration:** “The onboarding tour introduced every room and forgot to hand over the keys.”

This maps exposure to features onto access to a useful outcome. Follow it with the precise product action being discussed. The analogy helps explain that relationship; it does not prove the cause of abandonment or make every interface comparable to a house.

### Clearly fictional dialogue

**Illustrative dialogue, not a client quotation:**

> “Is the export automatic?”
>
> “Completely. Someone just has to start it.”

The reversal depends on two meanings of automation. For P14, recognition may be the whole purpose. If a practical conclusion follows, distinguish scheduled execution from other automated steps and support any capability claim separately. Never present invented dialogue as a transcript of an actual customer exchange.

### Escalation within a clear frame

**Illustration:** “The naming meeting produced three labels, two owners and a follow-up meeting about ownership.”

The sequence increases a recognisable process problem. It can work as an explicitly imagined scene. If represented as history, every detail requires confirmation. Do not add another invented escalation just because the real account lacks a stronger ending.

## 6. Place humor according to the post's job

| Pattern | Possible role | Keep unambiguous |
|---|---|---|
| P01, P06, P08: observation, research, measured case | Brief recognition around the problem or process. | Result, denominator, source, causal status and limitation. |
| P02, P03: decision or changed mind | A confirmed author habit, restrained scene detail or self-recognition. | What actually happened, what was remembered and what changed. |
| P04, P05, P10: process, checklist, comparison | Explain friction or a trade-off through an analogy. | Instructions, selection criteria and material failure conditions. |
| P09, P12: release or offer | Optional light voice around the situation. | Availability, scope, price, commitments and next action. |
| P13, P14: observation or professional humor | Recognition can be the primary payoff. | Whether the scene is fictional and whether a factual claim is being made. |

The target matters. Process contradictions can provide material without turning customers or colleagues into caricatures. The audience's relationship with the author, the cultural context and the stakes still require judgment. A joke appropriate in a familiar team may read differently in a public professional feed.

Humor can end at the payoff. Adding a moral, lesson and sales invitation after every joke may create three competing endings. For a teaching post, return to the explanation. For P14, a complete moment of recognition can be enough.

## 7. Revise for meaning, not merely a stronger punchline

**Weak fictional claim:** “Our AI is so good that reviewers are obsolete.”

**Improved fictional illustration:** “The demo was fluent. The reviewer still had questions.”

The second line offers a restrained contrast without asserting an unsupported replacement capability. A substantive post would then explain which output properties the reviewer checked. Humor cannot stand in for that evidence.

**Weak fictional self-disclosure:** “I am terrible at decisions, which is reassuring for everyone working with me.”

**Improved fictional self-recognition:** “I had given every option a score. I had not decided which criterion mattered.”

The revised line identifies a specific reasoning problem that a P03 post can resolve. It avoids claiming global incompetence for a laugh. Its suitability still depends on the author's actual account and voice.

Keep a plain version beside the humorous version. For the second example, the plain point is: “The criteria needed priorities before the scores could guide the choice.” If the joke changes that point, obscures the limitation or invents an experience, revise it or use the plain version.

## 8. Review interest and credibility separately

Ask what makes the subject worth reading, whether the opening's promise is delivered and which details change understanding. Then check who or what the joke targets, whether the literal claim is clear, whether the audience needs hidden context and whether the founder recognises the voice.

Check the evidence again after a humorous edit. [LWT05: Hansen and Wänke, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20947772/) concerns perceived truth, not established truth; only the abstract was inspected. A vivid, memorable or fluent sentence still requires verification. Keep source attribution and measurement literal.

Use comprehension feedback when available: ask a reader to restate the factual point, identify the joke's target and name the unresolved condition. That can reveal ambiguity; one reader is not a representative audience study. Organic comparisons between posts also mix topic, timing, audience and distribution differences.

The completed edit should preserve a useful idea and an intelligible voice. It need not contain a joke, personal drama or a cliffhanger. Research records and access limits in the [source registry](source-registry.json) support this bounded method; they do not supply guaranteed reactions, trust or sales.
